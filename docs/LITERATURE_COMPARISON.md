## 1. What the papers actually measure

### Eaton & Theuer (2009)
The inventory covers 402 executions in 1982–2007, but statement content is
analyzed for the 305 people with last statements (pp. 333–334). Table 3's apology
rate, 32.5%, therefore uses 305, not 402. It is also not a rate for the current
project's differently selected 50-person sample.

Two raters coded absence, presence and uncertainty, retaining disagreement as
uncertainty and comparing ratings approximately every 50 records (p. 334).
Reported kappas range from .89 to .99 for the listed independently coded
variables. Peace is an exception: it was established through discussion, with
no independent-coding kappa (Table 3, p. 338).

The paper separates apology, remorse, forgiveness requests, responsibility,
admission of guilt, innocence, accident accounts and excuses. It explicitly
explains that admitting guilt need not imply taking responsibility
(pp. 334–335). Apology and forgiveness requests also have recorded recipients.
Consequently, neither separating these concepts nor identifying the addressee
can be claimed as new here.

Sincerity includes apparent earnestness or expressions of love/gratitude toward
the victim's family. Peace includes explicit self-description and the raters'
interpretation of appearing at peace (p. 335). The peace/apology analysis uses
156 classifiable records, excluding many others; it is correlational
(pp. 340–342). It does not show that apologizing causes peace. The authors
acknowledge subjective interpretation and the absence of voice, face and bodily
information (pp. 342–343).

**Implication:** retain our distinction between explicit self-report and a
reader's interpretation. Do not reuse their sincerity or peace categories as
validated measurements of a private state. Nor should their result be dismissed
as if they ignored uncertainty or had no separate responsibility categories.

### Zhuo & Chun (2026)
The paper describes an initial collection of 575 records in 1984–2021, identifies
256 attributed to inmates classified as White, and retains 198 after excluding
blank and formulaic entries (pp. 6–7). These are the authors' reported counts;
the source collection has not been independently rebuilt here. Oral and
advance-written statements are included. The analysis is explicitly restricted
to the TDCJ White classification, not a race-comparative design.

Iterative manual reading develops emotional-expression and evaluative categories.
The authors state an interpretive, pattern-oriented purpose rather than reporting
statistical reliability testing (p. 7). Table 1 contains 855 emotional expressions
and 273 evaluative remarks, totaling 1,128 identified expressions (p. 8).
The approximately 76% refers to 855/1,128 expressions, not 76% of people,
statements or all words. Repeated expressions can contribute to that distribution.

The analysis already covers continued family connection (pp. 9–10), apologies to
different audiences (pp. 11–13), forgiveness with religious admonition
(pp. 14–15), reformed self-description and intention qualification (pp. 17–18),
and responsibility alongside judicial criticism (pp. 19–20). Its framework
explicitly rejects treating statements as transparent access to stable private
traits (pp. 5–6).

Especially close examples are:
- Example 10 (pp. 14–15): Barefoot combines forgiveness with a warning of
  damnation. The authors discuss the mixture of reconciliation and moral judgment.
  Our #600 pressure reading is a related case, not discovery of that phenomenon.
- Example 15 (p. 18): Green's reformed/non-threatening self-description is from
  the same speaker as our #138.
- Example 19 (pp. 19–20): Mason's complaint about lawyers is from the same
  statement as our #219.
- Example 18 (p. 19): Duff-Smith combines responsibility and criticism of his trial,
  closely overlapping the broader questions raised by our #407 comparison.

**Implication:** family attachment, positive self-presentation, religion,
reconciliation and mixed moral positioning are not uncovered territory. Some
examples overlap at source level; our reading cannot be presented as independent
confirmation on wholly new material.

### Schuck & Ward (2008)
Their sequential and micro-level analysis of 283 statements directly precedes
this project's broad approach. Relevant locations include pp. 48–49, 55–57 and
61–62. They distinguish apology from guilt, allow accepted guilt alongside
opposition to capital punishment, and identify closing formulas across opposed
and accepting statements. Religious and relational interpretation is already
central. Our contextual examples elaborate these established questions rather
than displace them.

## Smith (2019): full-text check
Smith, R. A. (2019), *Framing Forgiveness: An Analysis from the Offender's Point
of View*, Review of Religious Research 61(4), 341–363,
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13644-019-00385-y). The supplied full article was
read on 7 November 2026. Printed pages match PDF pages by adding 340 to the
PDF page number (for example, PDF page 8 is printed p. 348).

### Data, units and coding
The paper reports 537 execution records, 429 oral statements and 115 statements
with explicit forgiveness language (pp. 347–348). The author and two external
coders first conducted open coding of the larger set; agreement is reported as
exceeding 90%, followed by consensus on ambiguities. This is not a reported
Cohen's kappa. Thirteen advance-written statements are excluded according to
the footnote on p. 348; this differs from Zhuo & Chun's inclusion of written
and oral material.

Table 1 (p. 350) uses N=115, not all 429 oral statements or all 537 execution
records. Its six categories are nonexclusive: seeking others' forgiveness in a
religious context, seeking God's forgiveness for oneself, forgiving others in a
religious context, asking God to forgive others, and nonreligious seeking and
granting of forgiveness. Percentages should not be read as an exclusive
partition or as psychological-state prevalence. The text explicitly allows
multiple forgiveness expressions in one statement (p. 348).

### Findings directly overlapping our interpretation
The paper explicitly separates forgiveness from reconciliation (p. 343).
It discusses requests for forgiveness and religious meaning (pp. 352–353),
forgiving others while accusing them and religious warnings to victims'
relatives (pp. 353–354), and an offender's position as someone also claiming
injury (p. 360). These are direct precedents for our case discussion, including
the distinction between attempted repair and achieved reconciliation.

The examples include wording matching passages in our #433 (hope/pray that
others forgive) and #413 (repeated petitions to the Lord), pp. 352–353. Since
examples are not identified there by execution number, this is recorded as
matching text, not a verified reconstruction of every source ID. Neither these
passages nor the religious-pressure interpretation are treated as independent
new discoveries in our report.

### Specific methodological difference worth examining
Smith codes forgiveness as religious when embedded in an explicitly religious
narrative, while the nonreligious definition requires no accompanying religious
expression anywhere in the narrative (p. 348). This is a statement-context
classification. It does not, by itself, mean that each request contains a direct
religious obligation or that each utterance has one psychological function.

Our complete-text memos can distinguish local functions: God as judge (#508),
petitions for mercy (#413), a religious condition on another person's forgiveness
(#600), or a hoped-for afterlife encounter (#595). This offers a possible
contextual elaboration of an established framework. It is not evidence that
Smith's categories are wrong or that his entire analysis ignores local meaning.
No recoding or new religious/nonreligious frequency comparison has been done.

Religious coping theory supplies interpretations of function, not a measured
causal effect on fear, peace or intimacy in these archived statements. The
paper also notes that the witness-policy interpretation lacks control for
potential confounders (pp. 351, 360). No policy-effect estimate is imported into
this project.

## 3. Focused next analysis: relational repair and the boundaries of forgiveness
Keep the overall psychological-expression question. Within it, use a more
specific working question for the next comparison:

**How do people facing execution use requests for, and declarations of,
forgiveness to express self-reproach, sustain relationships and negotiate
responsibility?**

Chinese: 面对执行时，人们如何通过请求或给予宽恕，表达自责、维系关系并协商责任？

This is a focused extension question, not a demonstrated gap. The full Smith (2019) analysis is
a direct precedent for forgiveness direction, religious framing and the
forgiveness/reconciliation distinction. The 2026 paper's
close example must remain part of the argument. It does not replace the project
with a classification or sincerity-prediction task.

No new sample has been selected, and no additional owner annotation is needed to

forgiveness exchange.

## 4. Decision and remaining evidence
The immediate outcome is a literature-grounded qualitative comparison on the
completed reading set. Its practical value is interpretive precision: describe
what a statement does and what it leaves open, rather than assuming that love,
religion or apology proves one unified emotional state.

Smith's full methods have now been checked. A candidate extension is to
examine how religious references connect locally to forgiveness requests within
a complete statement. Broader literature checking is still needed before any
novelty claim; a difference in analytic granularity is not itself proof of a gap. These two
papers are enough to demonstrate strong overlap, not enough to prove the absence
of every narrower contribution.

For the current portfolio report, proceed with the focused comparison using
existing memos. If expanding into a new empirical study, specify why additional
cases are needed and how they might challenge the account before selecting them.
An independently recruited second reader would strengthen a later study, but
repeating the owner's completed 20-case review is not required now. Frequency
estimates would need a separate coding and sampling plan.

### What is already established in the closest work
Smith (2018) organizes expressions around comfort/closeness to God, intimacy
with others, control, life transformation and meaning. More than one function
can occur within a statement. The initial coding involved the author and two
external coders; the paper reports agreement above 90% followed by consensus,
not a chance-adjusted coefficient. The account of five functions should not be
confused with five empirically independent psychological dimensions.

Smith (2020) explicitly uses the same record window and distinguishes 270
religious statements at identification from 269 in the stratified analysis.
Do not silently harmonize the denominators or assume that the supplied articles
identify the reason for that one-record difference. Meaning was dropped for
small cells, which is an analytic choice, not evidence that people lack meaning
concerns. Comparisons surrounding the victim-witness policy use different
people across periods; they do not establish individual change or an isolated
policy effect.

Malaga and Delgado's descriptive procedure moves from a whole-text reading to
meaning units, psychologically sensitive descriptions and a structure connecting
themes. Their discussion already considers different recipients. Table 2 places
a preparation expression under acceptance and a willingness expression under
resignation. This creates a useful point for comparison with our contextual
reading, where bare readiness is retained without inferring either state.
It is a difference in operational interpretation, not proof that the earlier
study is wrong. Their argument about rehabilitation and low social desirability
should not be imported as evidence of sincerity or behavioral reform here.

### Important distinctions from the coping theory
The five broad functions belong to the fuller theoretical/RCOPE framework;
the Brief RCOPE is a different, shortened assessment organized around positive
and negative coping. Pargament and colleagues explicitly caution that these
terms do not guarantee beneficial or harmful outcomes in every context.
Their review also emphasizes predominantly American Christian validation
samples and needed adaptation for nontheistic contexts.

Accordingly, a religious appeal that a reader experiences as pressure is not
automatically negative religious coping. The former concerns an interpersonal
appeal and its reception; the latter concerns a theoretical pattern of religious
struggle. Nor does a positive-sounding prayer demonstrate successful coping.
Our #600 reading can discuss a conditional forgiveness appeal and the owner's
reaction while leaving intention, the recipients' responses and psychological
outcome unresolved. This distinction is especially important before extending
the framework to Murphy's Buddhist practice.

### Checks and unresolved details
Smith (2018), printed p. 286, states 9% for meaning in the paragraph, whereas
Fig. 1 displays 3%. The supplied page was visually checked: this is not merely
text extraction. Do not choose one value or reproduce either as a settled
estimate without further clarification. The other headline percentages are
conditional on the religious subset, not all executions. These published
proportions are not directly comparable with our differently defined 50-record
annotation sample.

### Revised research decision
Retain the broad psychological-expression question and the completed 20-case
comparison. Use religious coping as a sensitizing framework for the local role
of an expression, not a mandatory codebook, mental-health score or explanation
of every statement. Distinguish the speaker's expressed needs, a statement's
interpersonal function, actual support arrangements and observed outcomes.

The defensible next writing product is a compact contextual account showing
where our readings agree with, refine or remain unable to adjudicate earlier
interpretations. The proposal that religious functions or addressee distinctions
are themselves new is not supported. Whether the narrower distinction between
forgiveness requests, moral obligation and reception constitutes a contribution
requires additional positioning; no publication gap is declared here.
No new annotation, neural-network training or policy regression follows from
these four readings. The Murphy audio is now optional context, not a blocker
for the main expression study.
