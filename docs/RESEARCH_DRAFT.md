# Responsibility, relationships and religious meaning at the point of execution

Working research draft, 8 November 2026

## Abstract
Published last statements offer a record of how people facing execution describe
themselves and address others. This exploratory study asks how feelings,
relationships and interpretations of responsibility and death are expressed in
context. It compares 20 purposively selected Texas candidate documents, reading
each retained text in full alongside evidence-linked memos and religious-coping,
phenomenological and discourse research. Apparent co-occurrence informed selection; the analysis examines the
different objects and addressees of those expressions. Acknowledgment of harm
does not settle intention or judgment of punishment. Relational appeals can maintain family identity without establishing
reconciliation. Religious references can invoke mercy, authority, obligation or
future connection rather than expressing a single emotional orientation.
Contrasting sparse statements also shows why readiness should not automatically
be interpreted as serenity or approval of execution. These observations elaborate
existing research rather than establish their prevalence or a new psychological
mechanism. Separately documented spiritual-support cases illustrate the value
of institutional context, while leaving effects on psychological experience
unresolved.

## Research question and rationale
**How do people facing execution express feelings, sustain relationships and make
sense of responsibility and death in their final statements?**

The interest is psychological expression: what speakers describe feeling, which
relationships they seek to maintain, and how they interpret their situation. These are
public, institutionally situated statements. They are neither clinical interviews nor
direct measurements of an otherwise inaccessible private state. The question therefore
requires attention to whom a feeling is attributed, what a claim concerns, and how a
passage relates to the rest of the statement.

Earlier research already identifies affection, spirituality, remorse and meaning-
making. Schuck and Ward (2008) analyze self-presentation and meaning construction.
Smith (2018) applies religious-coping functions to last statements; Smith (2019)
examines forgiveness, and Smith (2020) considers race/ethnicity and institutional
context. Malaga and Delgado (2020) offer a closely related phenomenological analysis.
Eaton and Theuer (2009) distinguish apology and remorse-related content, while Zhuo
and Chun (2026) examine emotion and moral positioning in a restricted corpus.

Our purpose is to examine how these concerns combine in particular statements and
where a psychological interpretation needs to remain open. It is not to claim that
their co-occurrence or religious functions have gone unstudied. The [literature
comparison](LITERATURE_COMPARISON.md) records definitions, denominators, page
references, overlapping material and reading scope.

## Material and interpretive procedure
The saved project inventory contains 603 execution records and 480 candidate
person-level documents. Earlier text preparation yields 479 prepared records
and 477 with nonzero TF-IDF features. These quantities describe different stages;
the present close reading uses candidate statements, not only modeled texts.

Twenty candidate documents were selected from an existing 50-person development sample
for contrast. Earlier categories and discussions informed selection, including
deliberate selection for apparent co-occurrence. Co-occurrence is therefore a
selection feature, not a new finding from these cases. The [selection
record](../reports/open_reading_selection_v1.json) and
[workbook](OPEN_READING_WORKBOOK_v1.md) identify the cases. The sample is purposive
and cannot estimate frequencies among the 480 documents or everyone executed. Two
source boundaries matter: #1 combines a media statement with separately marked spoken
words; the fear passage belongs to the former. #49 retains the prisoner's message
within a recorder-framed exchange with an attorney, whose reply is excluded from the
candidate text. The analysis uses these retained texts, not necessarily complete
execution-day utterances. Cases #138 and #219 also appear in Zhuo and Chun (2026), so
their reuse is not independent evidence. The eight development labels and previous NMF
results were preserved; no new psychological scale was fitted.

Selection and memo drafting were AI-assisted; the project owner discussed
interpretations and revised readings. Each candidate text was read in full. Memos
retain source-context hashes, exact passages, interpretations, alternatives and
revisions. The comparison follows two organizing questions: what is accepted,
explained or contested about responsibility and judgment; and how relationships and
approaching death are described. These are interpretive lenses rather than exclusive
categories. Single-reader reactions are retained as reflexive notes, not generalized
audience responses. No second independent coding or new reliability estimate was
undertaken for this reading.

## Findings
### Acknowledgment does not resolve every question of responsibility
In #508, the speaker accepts responsibility while describing the death as an
accident and rejecting murder. The text supports distinguishing responsibility
for a loss from intention and characterization of the act. In #595, denial of intended harm and acknowledgment of wrong choices occur
in different addresses; the latter follows words to wife and daughters and
may concern departure or family roles rather than the victim's death. #600
combines involvement with uncertain knowledge and a conditional offer of blame.
The sleep and car references in #600 also offer a possible nonintentional
account, not simply uncertain memory. These readings remain distinct from an
unqualified innocence assertion.

Judgment of punishment is another question. #407 acknowledges wrongdoing and
willingness to bear a penalty but questions its effects on loved ones. #138 presents
religious change and good prison conduct while challenging execution's purpose. By
contrast, #58 explicitly asserts innocence. The analysis preserves these differences
without adjudicating the underlying legal cases.

### Connection can be sustained in speech without reconciliation
In #407, the speaker addresses an uncle whose forgiveness he says is unavailable,
then insists: “I am still your nephew”. This reasserts kinship without supplying
the uncle's response or proving that the relationship has been repaired.

In #595, the turn from the victim's family to wife and daughters changes the object of
apology toward leaving or giving up on family roles. Some referents remain uncertain.
#433 promises continuing presence for a loved one, while #49 retains a message of love
to a mother from a larger recorded exchange. Such language can maintain a bond in
speech, but neither sincerity nor deliberate image management follows from affection
alone. Requests for forgiveness also do not establish that forgiveness or
reconciliation occurred, a distinction already central to Smith (2019).

### Religious meaning is locally specific
Religious appeals take different positions in the accounts. In #413, petitions
for forgiveness accompany explicit attention to injury caused. In #508, divine
knowledge can support the speaker's account. In #153, mercy, reunion and offering
death for others' conversion give departure a religious meaning. #600 invokes
a religious consequence of withholding forgiveness. The owner experienced this
as pressure, but the recipients' experience and the speaker's intention remain
unknown.

Smith's religious-coping framework helps interpret these functions without requiring a
fresh codebook. It does not establish that an appeal achieved its purpose. Pargament,
Feuille and Burdzy (2011) distinguish religious struggle from supportive coping, while
warning against assuming outcomes from category names. Reader-perceived pressure is
not automatically their construct of negative religious coping. Their questionnaire
also cannot be scored by treating unmentioned experiences in a statement as absent.

### Readiness leaves important psychological questions unanswered
#15 says: “I have no last words. I am ready.” It supplies little basis for naming
an emotion. #11 adds deservedness, while #58 combines readiness with rejection
of the execution. The same closing function therefore does not settle approval,
resignation or calm. Malaga and Delgado's placement of preparation language
under acceptance provides a concrete comparison in operational interpretation;
our reading retains the narrower claim when context is insufficient.

Emotional attribution also matters. #508 describes his own peace; #32 wishes peace for
another person. #1 distinguishes lack of fear about the body from fear directed toward
God; awe and concern about divine judgment remain alternative readings, not an
established emotional category. The fear passage is in the media statement, not the
separately marked spoken portion. #219 and #583 preserve injustice claims as their
statements end. Together these cases resist reducing approaching death to one positive
or negative state. Silence about a feeling is not evidence that it was absent.

## Discussion and limits
The central result is a contextual account of psychological expression in which
responsibility, relationships, religion and judgments of punishment need not
point in the same direction. Their coexistence need not indicate inconsistency.
A change of addressee or object can explain why an apology, a protest and an
expression of love appear together.

This account agrees with substantial prior work. Its value is the traceable comparison
of retained documents, explicit alternative readings and sparse cases that limit
interpretation. It is not a demonstrated field-wide novelty, a validated measure of
mental state, or a causal test of religious care or capital-punishment policy.

Selection from development material, prior exposure, one owner's interpretive
participation, recording and translation boundaries, and the absence of listeners'
responses constrain conclusions. A final public statement supplies no within-person
trajectory and cannot establish private sincerity, actual reform, or effects of
prolonged confinement. Different people across calendar periods do not constitute a
longitudinal psychological study. Further work should address a specific missing
source or comparison rather than add model complexity to compensate for these
limitations.

## Appendix: institutional context
The Ramirez case links a documented accommodation request and judicial decision,
an attributed pre-execution interview, a report of pastoral prayer and touch at
execution, and an official last statement. The statement does not describe the arrangements for prayer and touch. Its
good-fight phrase has unresolved scriptural, idiomatic and legal readings; it
should not be treated as proof of nonreligious expression. Murphy's 2019 litigation provides
a preference contrast, including counsel's account of a request against touch.
His scheduled executions were stayed; this is not an untreated executed control.

These external cases are detailed in the [report](PSYCHOLOGICAL_EXPRESSION_REPORT.md).
They are not added to the 20-case denominator. They help distinguish desired support,
institutional permission, actual provision and attributed experience. Neither
establishes the effect of support on fear, peace or acceptance.
