# Responsibility, relationships and religious meaning in twenty Texas last statements: an exploratory contextual reading

Working research draft, 8 November 2026

## Abstract
Published last statements record how people facing execution address others and describe themselves. This exploratory study reads 20 purposively selected Texas candidate documents in full, with evidence-linked memos, and compares the readings with religious-coping, phenomenological and discourse research. Apparent co-occurrence of expressions informed case selection, so the analysis examines the different objects and addressees of those expressions. Acknowledging harm does not settle intent or judgment of punishment; appeals to family can maintain kinship without establishing reconciliation; religious references can invoke mercy, authority, obligation or future connection; and bare readiness should not be read as serenity or approval. Case selection and memo drafting were AI-assisted. One additional reader's open-ended responses to ten documents provide an exploratory contrast, preserving disagreement and emotional engagement rather than a reliability score. Source boundaries distinguish retained words from recorder framing, editorial additions and differently situated statements. The observations elaborate existing research and do not establish prevalence, a new mechanism or private mental state.

## Research question and existing research
**How do people facing execution express feelings, sustain relationships and make
sense of responsibility and death in their final statements?**

This draft addresses the question by interpretive illustration on 20 purposively
selected documents. It does not describe the range of expression or its frequency.
The interest is psychological expression: what speakers describe feeling, which
relationships they seek to maintain, and how they interpret their situation.
Attention to the experiencer, addressee and object of a claim guides the reading.

Earlier research identifies affection, spirituality, remorse and meaning-making.
Schuck and Ward (2008) analyze self-presentation and meaning construction.
Smith (2018) applies religious-coping functions to last statements; Smith (2019)
examines forgiveness, and Smith (2020) considers race/ethnicity and institutional
context. Malaga and Delgado (2020) offer a closely related phenomenological
analysis. Eaton and Theuer (2009) distinguish apology and remorse-related
content, while Zhuo and Chun (2026) examine emotion and moral positioning in a
restricted corpus. The [literature comparison](LITERATURE_COMPARISON.md) records
methods, denominators, page references and overlapping material.

## Materials, source boundaries and procedure
### Materials and selection
The saved inventory contains 603 execution records and 480 candidate person-level
documents. Earlier preparation yields 479 prepared records and 477 with nonzero
TF-IDF features. The close reading uses candidate documents, not only modeled texts.

Twenty documents were purposively selected from an existing 50-person development
sample. Earlier categories and discussions informed selection, including deliberate
selection for apparent co-occurrence. The [selection record](open_reading_selection_v1.json)
and [workbook](OPEN_READING_WORKBOOK_v1.md) identify the cases. The eight development
labels and previous NMF results remain unchanged background material.

Reader A is the project owner, whose guided readings underlie the memos. Reader B
is an additional human reader who later responded to ten of these documents.
The ten-document subset was purposive, not randomly sampled: it included all four
especially brief cases (#15, #11, #32, #49), together with #508, #413, #595, #407,
#1 and #600 for contrasts in responsibility, relationships and religious expression.
This describes the subset's composition, not a preregistered sampling rule or an
independent test set. Its [reading conditions](reader_response_context_v1.json)
and [original feedback](reader_response_v1.txt) are preserved.

### Interpretive procedure
Selection and memo drafting were AI-assisted; Reader A discussed interpretations
and revised readings. Each retained text was read in full. Memos record exact
passages, source-context hashes, interpretations, alternatives and revisions.
Two organizing questions concern what is accepted, explained or contested about
responsibility and judgment, and how relationships and approaching death are
described. These are interpretive lenses rather than exclusive categories.
Reader A's emotional responses are retained as reflexive notes. Reader B's later
response is an additional exploration described below, not fixed-label second coding.

## Findings
### Acknowledgment does not resolve every question of responsibility
In #508, the speaker accepts responsibility while describing the death as an
accident and rejecting murder. The text supports distinguishing responsibility
for a loss from intention and characterization of the act. In #595, denial of intended harm and acknowledgment of wrong choices occur
in different addresses; the latter follows words to wife and daughters and
may concern departure or family roles rather than the victim's death. #600
combines involvement with uncertain knowledge and a conditional offer of blame.
The sleep and car references in #600 also offer a possible nonintentional
account, not simply uncertain memory. These readings remain distinct from an
unqualified innocence assertion.

Judgment of punishment is another question. #407 acknowledges wrongdoing and
willingness to bear a penalty but questions its effects on loved ones. #138 (also Zhuo & Chun, Example 15) presents
religious change and good prison conduct while challenging execution's purpose. By
contrast, #58 explicitly asserts innocence. The analysis preserves these differences
without adjudicating the underlying legal cases.

### Connection can be sustained in speech without reconciliation
In #407, the speaker addresses an uncle whose forgiveness he says is unavailable,
then insists: “I am still your nephew”. This reasserts kinship without supplying
the uncle's response or proving that the relationship has been repaired.

In #595, the turn from the victim's family to wife and daughters changes the object of
apology toward leaving or giving up on family roles. Some referents remain uncertain.
#433 promises continuing presence for a loved one, while #49 retains a message of love
to a mother from a larger recorded exchange. Such language can maintain a bond in
speech, but neither sincerity nor deliberate image management follows from affection
alone. Requests for forgiveness also do not establish that forgiveness or
reconciliation occurred, a distinction already central to Smith (2019).

### Religious meaning is locally specific
Religious appeals take different positions in the accounts. In #413, petitions
for forgiveness accompany explicit attention to injury caused. In #508, divine
knowledge can support the speaker's account. In #153, mercy, reunion and offering
death for others' conversion give departure a religious meaning. #600 invokes
a religious consequence of withholding forgiveness. Reader A experienced this
as pressure, but the recipients' experience and the speaker's intention remain
unknown.

Smith's religious-coping framework helps interpret these functions without requiring a
fresh codebook. It does not establish that an appeal achieved its purpose. Pargament,
Feuille and Burdzy (2011) distinguish religious struggle from supportive coping, while
warning against assuming outcomes from category names. Reader-perceived pressure is
not automatically their construct of negative religious coping. Their questionnaire
also cannot be scored by treating unmentioned experiences in a statement as absent.

### Readiness leaves important psychological questions unanswered
#15 says: “I have no last words. I am ready.” It supplies little basis for naming
an emotion. #11 adds deservedness, while #58 combines readiness with rejection
of the execution. The same closing function therefore does not settle approval,
resignation or calm. Malaga and Delgado's placement of preparation language
under acceptance provides a concrete comparison in operational interpretation;
our reading retains the narrower claim when context is insufficient.

Emotional attribution also matters. #508 describes his own peace; #32 wishes peace for
another person. #1 distinguishes lack of fear about the body from fear directed toward
God; awe and concern about divine judgment remain alternative readings, not an
established emotional category. The fear passage is in the media statement, not the
separately marked spoken portion. #219 (also Zhuo & Chun, Example 19) and #583 preserve injustice claims as their
statements end. Together these cases resist reducing approaching death to one positive
or negative state. Silence about a feeling is not evidence that it was absent.

## Additional exploration: open-ended reader response
Reader A's guided readings underlie the memos; Reader B responded independently
of those interpretations to ten selected texts, using translation assistance.
According to Reader A's subsequent written confirmation, Reader B had not seen
or discussed the project's interpretations before responding. The translation
product and outputs were not recorded. Reader A intentionally prioritized authentic
intuitive responses rather than requiring completion of all packet questions.

The packet nevertheless prompted feelings, addressees, responsibility and alternative
readings. Overlap with the memos' lenses is therefore expected and is not independent
confirmation of those lenses. Reader B offers more elaborated interpretations of
some sparse texts: fatigue and withdrawal in #15, and a gift/acceptance reading of
#32 later qualified by uncertainty about this. In #1, concern about divine judgment
receives greater emphasis than awe. Such nuance-level comparisons are especially
sensitive to translation. Differences and self-corrections remain unresolved.

Our **analytic stance** is to preserve discomfort, tenderness and heaviness as
accounts of a reader's encounter, while distinguishing them from claims about
the speaker's state or motive. Reader B's response to the exchange in #49 and the
contrast between forgiveness appeals in #413 and #600 illustrate that stance;
the extended report preserves the ten-case comparison. One additional reader
cannot show how audiences generally respond. No agreement rate is computed.

## Discussion and limitations
The contextual comparison distinguishes the objects and addressees of responsibility,
relational and religious expressions. A change of addressee or object can help
explain why apology, protest and love occur together. Their co-occurrence was a
selection feature, so it is not an independent finding of this study.

The account agrees with substantial prior research. Its value is the traceable
comparison of retained documents, alternative readings and sparse cases that limit
interpretation. It does not establish a new mechanism, population prevalence or a
validated measure of private mental state. Selection from development material,
Reader A's prior exposure, guided interpretation, translation and recording boundaries,
and the absence of original addressees' responses constrain conclusions. A final
statement supplies no within-person trajectory and cannot establish sincerity,
behavioral reform or effects of prolonged confinement. Different people across
calendar periods do not constitute a longitudinal psychological study.

### What could change the interpretation?
A different reader's evidence-based account of the same passages could require
revision of a particular interpretation, especially an uncertain referent or the
object of responsibility. Disagreement alone would not establish that either reader
is correct. Applying the published rules of Eaton and Theuer or Smith to the same
material might change substantive summaries; such a comparison would need explicit
rule selection and treatment of ambiguity before results were inspected. Reading
additional, previously unexamined texts could also show that the present lenses
omit important concerns or fail to explain other cases. No such study has yet been
performed. These are possible challenges to particular readings and their usefulness,
not claims that basic logical distinctions can be disproved by a vote.

## Ethics and handling
The source material is publicly accessible on TDCJ pages. Analysis does not adjudicate
actual guilt, diagnose individuals or establish private sincerity. Victims and their
families are among those addressed, so descriptions avoid sensationalizing harm or
presenting a speaker's allegations as verified events. Public availability is not
assumed to remove the need for respectful contextual treatment.

Reader B is anonymized. Reader A confirmed in writing in this conversation that
Reader B agreed to anonymous paraphrase, direct quotation and publication of the
complete response; the [context record](reader_response_context_v1.json)
documents that confirmation. This is not represented as a separately signed consent
form from Reader B or as institutional ethics approval. Original wording and subsequent
self-corrections are retained, and the frozen corpus and labels are unchanged.

## Appendix: institutional context
The Ramirez record links an accommodation request and judicial decision, an attributed
pre-execution interview, a report of pastoral prayer and touch at execution, and an
official last statement. The statement does not describe the arrangements themselves.
Its good-fight phrase has unresolved scriptural, idiomatic and legal readings; it is
not proof of nonreligious expression. Murphy's 2019 litigation provides a preference
contrast, including counsel's account of a request against touch. His scheduled
executions were stayed; this is not an untreated executed control.

The [extended report](PSYCHOLOGICAL_EXPRESSION_REPORT.md) gives the sources and
attribution limits. These records are not pooled with the 20 documents. They distinguish
desired support, permission, provision and attributed experience without establishing
effects on fear, peace or acceptance.
